2The tools — mean, median, middle-50%

Three ways to compress many numbers into few. Read these together — in a minute you'll run them on your own data.

Mean (average)

x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) / n

Add every value, divide by the count. It is the balance point of the dots — so every value pulls on it, including a wild one.

Median

sort → take the middle

Sort the numbers; take the middle one (odd n), or the average of the two middle ones (even n). Only position matters — a wild value can barely move it.

Middle-50% width

sort → drop top & bottom 25% → width

How tightly the "typical" half of your trials bunch together. Small width = consistent. Pictured below.

Mean vs median — five numbers: 3, 5, 6, 9, 27

mean = (3+5+6+9+27)/5 = 10 — dragged toward 27. median = middle of the sorted list = 6 — didn't care. One wild value moved the balance point, not the middle position.

Middle-50% width — eight trials, sorted: 218, 224, 231, 236, 241, 249, 262, 305

Sort your 8 trials, cross out the 2 fastest and the 2 slowest (the lucky and unlucky extremes), and measure the width of the 4 that remain. That width is your consistency score — smaller = steadier. The plot in Step 3 draws this band automatically when you press Reveal.

3Look before you compute

Every dot is one trial. Same scale for both players — that is the honest way to compare. Talk first: where is each player's "pack"? Any dot far away from its pack (an outlier)? Who looks faster? Who looks more consistent? (Ignore the dashed 280 ms line for now — it belongs to Step 4's game.)

Before revealing: say your guess for your own median out loud.

Now toggle exclude flagged trials on and off. Watch which number jumps and which barely moves. Why? That difference is the whole story of mean vs median.

4The showdown

A tournament has one rule: react under 280 ms in every round. One slow round and you're out — your other rounds don't matter.

Look at the plot (the dashed line marks 280 ms). Whom do you enter — the player with the better average, or the more consistent one? Argue from the dots, not from pride.

Why 280 ms? No magic — we chose it: a bit slower than a typical adult click (~250 ms), so most trials beat it but not all. The world is full of chosen cutoffs like this — passing grades, speed limits, medical test thresholds. Someone picks the line; data decides who crosses it.

5Claim — Evidence — Limitation

Why this step? Making a chart is easy; the rare skill is saying what the data shows and what it can't show. This is how scientists end every analysis — and how you'll end every week of this program. By week 12 it will be automatic.

Problem 1 · New concept — the sneeze

Mia's 7 reaction times (ms): 231, 244, 219, 238, 226, 402, 235. The 402 happened when she sneezed.

(a) Find the mean and the median of all 7.   (b) Remove the 402 and find both again.   (c) Which summary moved more — and explain exactly why, using how each one is calculated.

Hints

① Sort the numbers first.

② The mean uses every value's size; the median only uses the middle position.

③ How much can one huge value pull a 7-number sum? How far can it move a middle position?

Problem 2 · Judgment — what a mean can hide

(a) After 20 trials, a player's mean is exactly 250 ms. One more trial makes the 21-trial mean 252 ms. Find the 21st time.

(b) Two players enter the showdown (out on the first round over 280 ms). On each future round, independently: Player A always records between 240 and 260 ms; Player B records 200 ms with probability ½ and 300 ms with probability ½. Both have mean 250. What is the expected number of rounds B plays, up to and including the round that eliminates him? Whom do you enter — and what does this say about what a mean can hide?

Hints

① Mean × count = total. Compare the totals before and after trial 21.

② Each round is a coin flip for B: heads = safe 200, tails = 300, out. On average, how many flips until the first tails?

③ Can A ever be eliminated at all?
